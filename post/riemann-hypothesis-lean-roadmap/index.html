A roadmap of the claimed Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis

A reading guide to the 175,000-file Lean development released on 20 November 2026 that claims RH and GRH for Dirichlet L-functions: what is standard, what carries the argument, where to look first, and a plan for human readers.
November 20, 2026 · 4 min · 840 words · PeaBrane

On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge released a Lean 4 development, made with Claude, that claims a formal proof of the Riemann hypothesis and of GRH for Dirichlet L-functions. It has about 175,000 files and 17 million lines, and no comments. I have written a reading guide to it: what the argument is, which parts are standard background, where the weight rests, and how human readers could split up the work of understanding and checking it.

Read it: Zenodo, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24405360 · PDF

Status. At the time of writing, no independent check of the development had been reported. I did not build it, and the note makes no claim that the proof is correct. It reads the development’s definitions and theorem statements and the author’s four-page remark. Every step is tied to a frozen file and line at a pinned commit of a byte-exact public mirror.

At a glance#

  • About 81% of the lines re-prove standard or published mathematics: étale cohomology, resolution of singularities, p-adic Hodge theory, arithmetic intersection theory, theta correspondences, automorphy lifting. All of this can be set aside.
  • About 19% is candidate new argument.
  • About 5% (≈0.85 million lines) is the core the note maps.
  • The top-level Formalism/Statement*.lean files are abandoned designs, never proved or used. Don’t start reading there.

The argument in one paragraph#

As I read it, the development runs Stepanov’s method over ℤ. Stepanov proved the Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields with three ingredients: Frobenius makes high-order vanishing free at the points being counted, a degree bound turns vanishing into a count, and a multiplicity parameter is traded against the global cost. The development replaces each of these:

  • Frobenius. At primes inert in a CM field, integral theta-function periods are forced to be divisible by a large power of p. Summed over p, those exponents form a Riesz mean of Λ(n)(1−χ(n)).
  • Degree bound. An averaged Bost slope inequality from Arakelov geometry. The archimedean side of the same objects (Stirling and the Chowla–Selberg period) is exactly the explicit-formula main term, so the inequality reads “prime sum ≤ its own main term + error”.
  • Multiplicity. Over ℤ there is no derivative to raise it. The development instead lifts m ≈ √x times between definite unitary groups by theta correspondence. Each lift adds one copy of the signal, while the global costs are paid once. The error x/m + m is smallest at m ≈ √x, and that is where the exponent 1/2 comes from.

Finite Fourier analysis over an auxiliary abelian field and a windowed form of Landau’s theorem then turn the one-sided bound into zero-free half-planes.

How the claimed theorem is proved, as I read it. Shaded boxes are ingredients that look new; double-bordered boxes are known results or methods that the development adapts, strengthens or re-proves. Tags such as [II1] refer to the reading plan below.

Where to look first#

These are not findings of error. They are where, in my reading, the weight of the argument rests.

  1. Forced divisibility. The claim is that Bessel periods of m-fold iterated theta lifts are divisible by the inert-prime “factorial excess”, on a sublattice of small colength. I know of no published analogue. Along one code path the relevant clause holds only by definition, so check the binding to a real valuation first.
  2. Sharpness. The slope inequality must be exact to relative error x−1/2, uniformly over unitary groups of rank polynomial in x and fields of degree about x².
  3. The Selmer bound. It needs a bound on Selmer groups of Dirichlet characters that is uniform in ℓ. That is a theorem of independent weight; the author describes it as a significant weakening of Vandiver’s conjecture.
  4. The covolume bound. It needs arithmetic proportionality on general-signature unitary Shimura varieties, which the literature has in special cases only.
  5. Two weakened inputs. The slots named for local Langlands and for Caraiani’s local–global compatibility are filled by weaker statements.

A plan for human readers#

The note proposes eleven write-up targets in three tiers, each with its Lean entry points and what to check first:

  • Tier I, self-contained analytic statements. These are short and need only analytic number theory, so they make good first targets:
    • [I1] the windowed one-sided Landau theorem;
    • [I2] the balance identity;
    • [I3] the deduction to the level-sum bound;
    • [I4] the choice of auxiliary conductors by second moments.
  • Tier II, the arithmetic heart. These decide whether the method works:
    • [II1] forced divisibility, with a CM-curve warm-up;
    • [II2] the slope inequality with frame defects;
    • [II3] the archimedean budget;
    • [II4] near-orthogonality from a relative trace formula.
  • Tier III, inputs of independent weight. Each could be a paper; start with a statement-level audit:
    • [III1] the Selmer bound;
    • [III2] the covolume bound;
    • [III3] the Galois-representation and local inputs.

Suggested order: [I1], [I2] and the cheap audit [III3]; then the warm-up for [II1], because if that fails nothing downstream matters; then [I3] with [II3]; then the rest.
